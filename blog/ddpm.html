Generative models

Diffusion models

Junyu Chen22 October 2026

A motivating example

A limit order book lists the prices at which traders are willing to buy and sell, and how much. For trading and risk management we would like to forecast the order book a little ahead. A single best guess is not enough: the market may stay calm, or liquidity may suddenly dry up, and the second case is the one risk managers worry about. What we want is the whole range of possible futures, with their probabilities.

In our paper on order book forecasting, we produce such forecasts with a conditional diffusion model: it draws many possible future order books, each of them realistic. This post explains the model underneath, the denoising diffusion probabilistic model, or DDPM (Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020).

The setup

We have examples \(x_0\) from an unknown distribution: each \(x_0\) could be an order book, a day of returns or an image. We want a machine that produces new draws from the same distribution.

In the figures \(x_0\) has just two coordinates, a price change and a bid–ask spread (both standardised), and comes in two kinds: calm days, with small price changes and narrow spreads, and stressed days, with falling prices and wide spreads. About 30% of days are stressed.

The idea of DDPM fits in one sentence: slowly turn data into noise, then train a network to undo it, one small step at a time.

1Turning data into noise

The forward process takes a data point and, over \(T\) steps, mixes in a little Gaussian noise at each step:

\[ x_t=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\;x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\;\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim N(0,I),\qquad t=1,\dots,T. \]

The numbers \(\beta_t\) are small and fixed in advance. Ho et al. let them grow from 0.0001 to 0.02 over \(T=1000\) steps. The factor \(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\) shrinks the point slightly at each step, so the spread of the points stays under control while noise is added.

Why add noise at all? Because the end point is easy. After enough steps nothing is left of the data, and \(x_T\) is simply standard normal noise, which a computer can draw instantly.

If we can learn the way back, from noise to data, then drawing noise and walking back produces new data.

The jump in step 4 is what makes training fast. Sums of Gaussians are Gaussian, so the many small steps add up to one:

\[ x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim N(0,I),\qquad \bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s). \]

So \(x_t\) is a shrunken copy of the data plus noise. \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) says how much signal is left and \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\) how much noise has been added; they are the two curves in Figure 1.

2Training: guess the noise

To run the process backwards, the model has to answer one question at every step: given the noisy point \(x_t\), what noise was added? A neural network \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) is trained to answer it. Training repeats three simple moves:

  1. take a real data point \(x_0\) and a random step \(t\);
  2. draw noise \(\varepsilon\) and form the noisy point \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\);
  3. let the network guess \(\varepsilon\) from \(x_t\) and \(t\), and adjust its weights to reduce the squared error.

Guessing the noise is the same as guessing the clean point, because \(\hat x_0=\bigl(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon\bigr)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). Figure 2 shows the guesses as clean points, which are easier to picture.

It is just regression. The inputs are \(x_t\) and \(t\), the target is \(\varepsilon\) and the loss is the squared error. The only twist is that the training examples are made on the fly, by adding fresh noise to real data, so their supply is unlimited.

3Generating: walk back from noise

With a trained network, generating new data is a loop. Start from pure noise \(x_T\sim N(0,I)\) and, for \(t=T,\dots,1\), take one small step back:

\[ x_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_t}}\Bigl(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Bigr)+\sqrt{\beta_t}\;z,\qquad z\sim N(0,I). \]

The first part removes a little of the predicted noise. The second part adds back a little fresh noise, which keeps the samples spread out like the data instead of collapsing onto the average.

Why so many small steps? Over one small step the change is nearly Gaussian, which a network can learn well; one giant leap from noise to data would not be.

The price is speed: Ho et al. needed 1000 network evaluations per sample. Later methods such as DDIM reach similar quality in far fewer steps.

Back to the order book

For forecasting we want not just any realistic order book, but one that fits the current market. That only requires giving the network one more input: it becomes \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)\), where \(c\) is the recent history. The same loop then draws from the conditional distribution of the future given the past, many possible futures given today.

The spread of these samples is the forecast uncertainty, and the share of stressed scenarios is a risk estimate. For sequences such as order books, the network inside is often a Transformer.

In short

A diffusion model learns a distribution by learning to remove noise. The forward process slowly mixes data with Gaussian noise until only noise is left; it has no parameters, and any step can be reached in one jump. A network is trained, by plain least squares, to guess the noise that was added at each step. To generate, start from noise and repeatedly remove a little of the predicted noise while adding back a little fresh randomness. The result is not one forecast but many realistic samples, so rare outcomes such as a stressed market keep their probability instead of being averaged away.

The figures use a toy distribution with two clusters. Instead of a trained network they use the exact best guess for that distribution, so they show what a perfectly trained network would do. The noise schedule is the one of Ho, Jain and Abbeel: β grows linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02 over T = 1000 steps.

Reference: J. Ho, A. Jain and P. Abbeel (2020), "Denoising diffusion probabilistic models", Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems.
