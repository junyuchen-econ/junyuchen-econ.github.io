Financial econometrics

CoVaR

Junyu Chen23 October 2026

A motivating example

In September 2008 Lehman Brothers failed, and within days the trouble spread to other banks, money-market funds and insurers. Before the crisis, risk was mostly measured bank by bank. But a bank can look safe on its own and still drag the whole system down when it gets into trouble.

CoVaR, proposed by Tobias Adrian and Markus Brunnermeier, measures exactly this: the risk of the financial system given that one institution is in distress. The difference between that risk and the system's risk on a normal day, called ΔCoVaR, is the institution's contribution to systemic risk.

The setup

Let \(X^i\) be the daily return of institution \(i\), say a bank, and \(X^{\text{sys}}\) the return of the financial system, for example an index of all financial firms. Returns are in percent, so losses are negative numbers. Throughout, \(q\) is a small probability such as 5%.

1Value-at-Risk: one bank on its own

The Value-at-Risk of the bank is the return that is beaten on all but the worst \(q\) share of days:

\[ \Pr\bigl(X^i\le \text{VaR}^i_q\bigr)=q. \]

In words, it is the \(q\)-quantile of the bank's returns. With \(q=5\%\), the bank loses more than its VaR on one day in twenty.

Why 5%? Regulators traditionally look at \(q=1\%\). The figures use 5% so that the tail contains enough days to see; the slider goes down to 1%.

2CoVaR: the system when one bank is in trouble

CoVaR is the VaR of the system, computed only over the days on which the bank is in distress, that is, at its own VaR:

\[ \Pr\bigl(X^{\text{sys}}\le \text{CoVaR}^{\text{sys}|i}_q \;\big|\; X^i=\text{VaR}^i_q\bigr)=q. \]

To isolate what the bank adds, compare it with the same quantity on a normal day, when the bank is at its median return:

\[ \Delta\text{CoVaR}^{\text{sys}|i}_q=\text{CoVaR}^{\text{sys}\,|\,X^i=\text{VaR}^i_q}_q-\text{CoVaR}^{\text{sys}\,|\,X^i=\text{Median}^i}_q. \]

The more negative ΔCoVaR, the more the system suffers when this bank is in trouble.

Risky is not the same as systemic. A bank with a large VaR need not have a large ΔCoVaR, and the other way round. Adrian and Brunnermeier find that across institutions the two are only loosely related, which is why supervising each bank's own risk is not enough.

3Estimating CoVaR with quantile regression

In real data there are hardly any days on which a bank is exactly at its VaR, so CoVaR cannot be read off a band of days. Adrian and Brunnermeier estimate it with quantile regression: a line through the cloud with a share \(q\) of the days below it,

\[ (\hat\alpha_q,\hat\beta_q)=\arg\min_{\alpha,\beta}\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_q\bigl(X^{\text{sys}}_t-\alpha-\beta X^i_t\bigr),\qquad \rho_q(u)=u\,\bigl(q-\mathbb 1\{u<0\}\bigr). \]

Then CoVaR is the line evaluated at the bank's VaR.

Why not least squares? Least squares finds the average of the system's return for each bank return. Risk managers need the tail, and the tilted loss \(\rho_q\) moves the line down until only a share \(q\) of the days lies below it.

In practice Adrian and Brunnermeier also let the line depend on lagged state variables \(M_{t-1}\), such as market volatility and credit spreads, so that CoVaR moves with market conditions:

\[ X^{\text{sys}}_t=\alpha_q+\beta_q X^i_t+\gamma_q^{\top}M_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t . \]

Replacing the straight line by a single-index model, a neural network or a Transformer that also reads the news gives the methods in the second half of the timeline below.

4Two decades of CoVaR research

The main steps, from the newest to the original paper. Each entry says what it changed: the definition of distress, the way CoVaR is estimated, or the way forecasts are checked.

  1. 2026
    text and Transformers Transformer-based CoVaR: Systemic risk in textual information J. Chen, T. Boot, L. Kong and W. Wang · arXiv 2602.12490, revise and resubmit at the Journal of Financial Econometrics

    Estimates CoVaR with a Transformer that reads financial news alongside market data, using text embeddings from a large language model instead of ready-made sentiment scores. Error bounds show that the estimator can work with small samples, and with Reuters news from 2006 to 2013 the text improves out-of-sample CoVaR forecasts, most of all in times of market stress.

  2. 2026
    dynamic models Dynamic CoVaR modeling and estimation T. Dimitriadis and Y. Hoga · Journal of Business & Economic Statistics

    Builds dynamic models, called CoCAViaR, that forecast VaR and CoVaR together as market conditions change, and proves that their two-step estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal. For large US banks the forecasts beat existing benchmarks.

  3. 2024
    checking forecasts Backtesting systemic risk forecasts using multi-objective elicitability T. Fissler and Y. Hoga · Journal of Business & Economic Statistics 42(2), 485–498

    Shows that CoVaR cannot be backtested with a single scoring function, as is done for VaR, and introduces multi-objective elicitability to compare and validate CoVaR forecasts, including a traffic-light test.

  4. 2022
    neural networks Modelling systemic risk using neural network quantile regression G. Keilbar and W. Wang · Empirical Economics 62(1), 93–118

    Estimates CoVaR with neural network quantile regression and builds networks of risk spillovers from its marginal effects. Out of sample it beats a linear benchmark, a sign that non-linearity matters.

  5. 2018
    many covariates Single-index-based CoVaR with very high-dimensional covariates Y. Fan, W. K. Härdle, W. Wang and L. Zhu · Journal of Business & Economic Statistics 36(2), 212–226

    Lets CoVaR depend on a very large number of covariates by projecting them onto a single index, and shows good backtesting results for the US financial sector.

  6. 2016
    networks TENET: Tail-event driven network risk W. K. Härdle, W. Wang and L. Yu · Journal of Econometrics 192(2), 499–513

    Estimates tail spillovers among many US financial institutions with single-index quantile regression and variable selection, and turns them into a network that ranks institutions as emitters and receivers of systemic risk.

  7. 2014
    definition On dependence consistency of CoVaR and some other systemic risk measures G. Mainik and E. Schaanning · Statistics & Risk Modeling 31(1), 49–77

    Shows that when distress means being exactly at the VaR, CoVaR does not always rise as the dependence between institution and system increases, even for normally distributed returns. Defining distress as being at the VaR or worse fixes this.

  8. 2013
    definition Systemic risk measurement: Multivariate GARCH estimation of CoVaR G. Girardi and A. T. Ergün · Journal of Banking & Finance 37(8), 3169–3180

    Redefines distress as the institution being at or below its VaR rather than exactly at it, estimates CoVaR with a multivariate GARCH model, and makes the resulting forecasts testable against data.

  9. 2008
    the original CoVaR T. Adrian and M. K. Brunnermeier · first circulated as Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report 348 (2008); American Economic Review 106(7), 1705–1741 (2016)

    Defines CoVaR and ΔCoVaR, estimates them with quantile regressions on market data and state variables, and shows that ΔCoVaR is only loosely related to an institution's own VaR. Leverage, size, maturity mismatch and asset-price booms help predict it.

In short

VaR measures how badly one institution can do on its worst days. CoVaR measures how badly the whole financial system does on the days that institution is in distress, and ΔCoVaR, the gap to a normal day, is the institution's contribution to systemic risk. A risky bank need not be a systemic one: what matters is how strongly its bad days are tied to everyone else's. CoVaR is estimated with quantile regression, a line or curve with a share q of the days below it, and the research since 2008 has refined the definition of distress, made the regression flexible enough for many institutions, non-linear tails and news text, and developed ways to check the forecasts.

The figures use simulated returns: the bank's return is normal with a standard deviation of 2%, and the system follows the bank more closely, and with more spread, on the bank's bad days. Because the model is known, the "true" quantiles in Figures 2 and 3 are exact.
