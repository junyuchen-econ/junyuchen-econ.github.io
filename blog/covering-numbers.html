Covering numbers

Junyu Chen22 October 2026

A motivating example

Regulators want to know how much a bank could lose on a bad day when other banks are in trouble too. This number is called CoVaR, short for conditional value-at-risk. We can estimate it with a flexible model, such as a deep neural network, trained on a few years of daily returns and news.

A flexible model can fit the past almost perfectly. What we really want to know is whether its forecast for tomorrow can be trusted. That depends on how flexible the model is compared with how much data we have, and covering numbers are one of the standard ways to measure that flexibility.

1Why count functions?

A larger candidate set always lets fit the past data better. Past some point, though, the extra candidates are used to fit the noise, and predictions on new days get worse.

So how well a model fits tells us little on its own. We also need to know how many genuinely different functions it could choose from. That number is the covering number.

Figure 1 is a toy version of this problem with a single input . The candidates are step functions with steps, fitted to 30 noisy points from a sine curve. More steps means more candidates.

A finance analogy. Backtest enough trading strategies on past prices and one of them will look brilliant by luck alone. A model with many candidate functions is in the same position: some candidate will match the noise in the data. The covering number counts how many genuinely different candidates the model gets to try.

2What ε and a ball are

A fitted model only sees a function through its values at the data points. Two functions that agree at every data point look identical to it. Two that differ only slightly look almost identical.

Covering numbers turn this into a count. Choose a tolerance ε. Call two functions close if, at every data point, their values differ by at most ε. A ball is a representative function together with everything close to it. The covering number is the smallest number of balls that together contain every candidate.

With two data points this can be drawn: each function becomes a point in the plane, located by its two values. Figure 2 uses straight lines through the origin (one free parameter, the slope) and general straight lines (two free parameters).

Why not simply count the functions? The lines alone are infinitely many, one for every slope , so a plain count would be infinite for almost any model and tell us nothing.

Yet slopes 0.50 and 0.51 give almost the same predictions. The covering number lumps such nearly identical functions together and counts the groups instead. That number is finite, and it is larger when the model can make more truly different predictions.

Two takeaways carry over to real problems:

  1. ε is a resolution we choose, not part of the model. It decides how different two functions must be before we count them separately. A smaller ε notices finer differences, so the count goes up.
  2. More free parameters, faster growth. Each time ε is halved, the count roughly doubles with one parameter and quadruples with two. With parameters it is multiplied by about . Put differently, : the number of parameters times how fine the resolution is.

In short

A covering number counts how many genuinely different functions a model can choose from, at a tolerance ε that we pick. Error bounds need this count because a model with more choices can fit noise by luck. Functions are compared only at the data points, and two count as close when they are within ε at every point, which is why the balls are squares. The more free parameters, the faster the count grows as ε shrinks. In the CoVaR example, this count is what tells us whether a few years of data are enough to trust a flexible model.
