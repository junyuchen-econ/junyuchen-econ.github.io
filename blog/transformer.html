Transformers

Junyu Chen22 October 2026

A motivating example

News moves markets. A headline such as the bank raised rates after loan losses matters for bank risk; a story about a boat drifting to a river bank does not. To use news in a risk model, such as the CoVaR model from the post on covering numbers, the text first has to be turned into numbers.

The difficulty is that a word's meaning depends on the words around it. "Bank" means different things in the two sentences above. The Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) is a neural network built for exactly this: it lets every word look at every other word and update its own meaning. It is the architecture behind today's language models.

The setup

A sentence is split into tokens \(w_1,\dots,w_n\), which are roughly words. Each token gets a vector of numbers, its embedding \(x_i\in\mathbb R^d\), learned from data. Words with related meanings get nearby vectors.

On its own, \(x_i\) is the same in every sentence: "bank" always starts from the same vector. The Transformer's job is to turn these into new vectors in which each word has absorbed what it needs from the rest of the sentence:

\[ (x_1,\dots,x_n)\;\longmapsto\;(z_1,\dots,z_n). \]

The main tool for this is called attention.

1Attention: every word looks at the others

Attention builds each word's new vector as a blend of the words in the sentence. The blend weights say how much each word matters for it. To set the weights, every word plays three roles, each obtained by multiplying its embedding by a learned matrix:

  • a query \(q_i=W_Qx_i\): the question the word asks, such as "which words tell me what I mean?";
  • a key \(k_j=W_Kx_j\): the label a word shows to the others, used to match questions;
  • a value \(v_j=W_Vx_j\): the information a word hands over when it is chosen.

Figure 1 uses vectors with only two coordinates, so they can be drawn: how much a word is about finance, and how much it is about rivers and nature. To keep the picture simple, the keys and values are the embeddings themselves.

A library analogy. The query is what you type into the catalogue, the keys are the titles on the shelves and the values are the books' contents. An ordinary search returns the single best match. Attention returns a blend of all the books, weighted by how well each title matches.

All words do this at the same time. Stack the queries, keys and values of the sentence as the rows of matrices \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\). Then attention is one line,

\[ \operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Bigl(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt d}\Bigr)V, \]

with the softmax taken row by row. Two features make this powerful. Every word can look at every other word directly, however far apart they are; older models had to pass information along the sentence one word at a time. And it is just matrix products, which computers do quickly and in parallel.

In a real model the matrices \(W_Q\), \(W_K\) and \(W_V\) are not chosen by hand as in Figure 1. They are learned from data, so the model itself discovers which questions are worth asking.

2Word order needs a signal

Attention has a blind spot: the weights depend only on the vectors, not on where the words stand. "The bank sued the regulator" and "the regulator sued the bank" contain the same words, so attention gives "bank" exactly the same new vector in both. For risk, who sued whom matters.

The fix is to add a position vector \(p_i\) to each embedding before attention, so the model works with \(x_i+p_i\). The original Transformer builds \(p_i\) from sines and cosines that turn at different speeds:

\[ p_{i,2k}=\sin(i\,\omega_k),\qquad p_{i,2k+1}=\cos(i\,\omega_k),\qquad \omega_k=10000^{-2k/d}. \]

Each pair of coordinates behaves like the hand of a clock that turns by \(\omega_k\) per position. Figure 2 shows what this achieves.

Why not just write down the position number? Adding 1, 2, 3, … would grow without bound and swamp the word vectors, and a model trained on short texts would never have seen position 500.

Sines and cosines stay between −1 and 1 and repeat in a regular way, so the model can learn rules such as "look at the word just before me" that work at every position.

Many newer models learn their position vectors from data, or encode only the distance between two words. The idea is the same: attention has to be told where each word is.

3Putting it together

A Transformer combines attention with a few simple pieces into a block, and repeats the block several times. Figure 3 walks through one block from the bottom up.

How big are real models? The original Transformer had 6 blocks, 8 attention heads and vectors of 512 numbers, about 65 million parameters in total. Today's large language models use the same kind of block, many more of them, and billions of parameters.

In short

A Transformer turns a sequence of words into a sequence of vectors in which every word knows its context. The key step is attention: each word asks a question (its query), compares it with every word's label (its key), turns the matches into weights with a softmax, and takes the weighted average of their information (their values). Attention ignores order, so a position signal is added to each word. Attention, a small per-word network, skip connections and normalisation form a block, and blocks are stacked. In a text-based risk model, the result is a numerical summary of the news that a forecast such as CoVaR can use.

The vectors in Figures 1 and 2 are small hand-picked examples; real models learn vectors with hundreds of coordinates. The head patterns in Figure 3 are illustrations.
